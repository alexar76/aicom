Minimum Public Discovery Information: Requirements and Common Semantics
                      draft-cui-dawn-mdi-model-02
Abstract
The charter of the Discovery of Agents With Names (DAWN) working
   group asks it to specify the minimum public discovery information
   that a client obtains when it discovers an AI resource.  An
   individual draft on DAWN terminology defines Minimum Discoverable
   Information (MDI) as the minimum amount of information an entity
   needs to provide to become discoverable, distinguishes it from the
   Minimum Viable Dataset (MVD) that a discovery mechanism needs as
   input, and gives only examples of the content of either.  Individual
   drafts on the DAWN problem statement and requirements call for a
   standardized minimum structure and list what it must at least
   contain; in its absence, emerging discovery formats risk each
   defining its own minimal metadata, with descriptors that do not
   interoperate across organizational or protocol boundaries.

   This document addresses the minimum public discovery information as a
   client obtains it and as a publisher provides it, and its relation to
   the input of discovery; registration mechanisms are outside its
   scope.  Using the distinction RFC 3444 draws between information
   models and data models, it describes the information involved in
   publication, lookup, and response; proposes a procedure that derives
   what is minimal for each deployment context from the scope of the
   charter; applies the procedure to the charter's three deployment
   contexts: a local network, an organization, and the domain of a
   cooperating party; and proposes a set of elements with the minimal
   constraints that hold among them.  Information can be supplied by the
   discovery response, by context the client already has, or by binding
   rules.  This document is input to the DAWN architecture and protocol
   work; normative definitions that affect interoperability belong to
   the corresponding Standards Track documents.  It is a mapping target
   for richer descriptors.

1.  Introduction

   Clients often need to discover information about AI resources, such
   as AI agents, before interacting with them [DAWN-CHARTER], and often
   have no pre-configured relationship with them.  Before any
   interaction can begin, a client needs a small amount of information
   about the AI resource: what it is, what it does, how to reach it, and
   what assurances, if any, accompany those claims.
   [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology] defines Minimum Discoverable
   Information (MDI) as the minimum amount of information an entity
   needs to provide to become discoverable, and does not define its
   content; [I-D.king-dawn-requirements] requires each discovery
   mechanism to define the MDI, including at least the entity's type,
   the services it offers, its reachability information, and the
   communication protocols and any security parameters needed to
   interact with it, and to indicate whether discovery information is
   static or dynamic; [I-D.akhavain-moussa-dawn-problem-statement] calls
   directly for a standardized structure carrying the minimum
   information a discovery response returns; and
   [I-D.moussa-dawn-gap-analysis] sketches a provisional baseline
   without normalizing it.  Mechanism-specific drafts define such fields
   for their carriers ([I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns],
   [I-D.blwm-dawn-common-discovery-parameters]); none defines MDI
   content independently of a carrier.

   The DAWN charter [DAWN-CHARTER] places this information at the center
   of the working group's scope: discovery is limited to a client
   requesting and obtaining the minimum public discovery information for
   an AI resource (for example, its type, reachability information,
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 3]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
communication protocol, and services) within a local network, within
   an organization, or between cooperating parties with trust
   relationships over the public Internet.  The charter assigns this
   information to two deliverables: the Discovery Architecture captures
   the requirements behind it, and the Protocol specifications, possibly
   one per deployment context, define it.  This document uses "MDI" as
   shorthand for the information the charter calls minimum public
   discovery information; where the two might differ, the charter's
   wording governs.

   Meanwhile, the gap is being filled independently and incompatibly by
   adjacent ecosystems: DNS-based bootstrap
   ([I-D.mozleywilliams-dnsop-dnsaid]), well-known JSON manifests, agent
   cards, and signed capability documents each carry a slightly
   different minimal field set with slightly different semantics, each
   bound to its own substrate.  A descriptor that is minimal and
   sufficient in one system is unreadable in another.

   [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology] distinguishes the input of discovery,
   the Minimum Viable Dataset (MVD), from MDI, which it defines by what
   an entity provides to become discoverable and describes as what a
   lookup returns.  This document considers MDI at both points,
   publication and response, and relates it to the input.  Following the
   distinction [RFC3444] draws between information models and data
   models, it describes the information involved in discovery
   (Section 5), proposes how to decide what is minimal (Section 6),
   applies that procedure to the charter's three deployment contexts
   (Section 7), and describes the elements of the common discovery
   header and the minimal constraints that hold among them (Section 8),
   leaving encodings to bindings.  Section 13 lists the points on which
   input is sought.

1.1.  Requirements Language

   The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT",
   "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and
   "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in
   BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all
   capitals, as shown here.

2.  Terminology

   This document uses the terms defined in
   [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology], including AI Resource, Entity,
   Discovered Entity, Discovering Entity, Discovery, Discovery
   Information, Discovery Scope, Discoverable Object, Capability Card,
   Trust Indicator, and, centrally, Minimum Discoverable Information
   (MDI); readers are assumed to be familiar with that document.  The
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 4]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
elements defined here are the normalized slots through which a
   minimal subset of an entity's Properties is conveyed as Discovery
   Information.

   The entity that MDI describes is a Discovered Entity, which in DAWN
   is an AI system or AI resource [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology]; the
   charter's AI resource "may be an individual named agent or a service
   such as a catalog that will further the discovery process"
   [DAWN-CHARTER].  In this document, "resource" is short for AI
   resource.  The client, as the charter calls it, is the Discovering
   Entity; this document also calls it the discovering party.  In
   addition:

   Discovery response:  What a discovery mechanism returns to a client;
      it may carry element values for one or more entities.

   Known context:  Information the client already has when it interprets
      a discovery response, such as the Discovery Scope it searched, the
      name it started from, or the network it is attached to.  Some of
      it is part of the MVD (Section 5).

   Minimum Viable Dataset (MVD):  As defined in
      [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology], "the data that must be provided to
      the discovery mechanism to enable it to return indications of
      Discovered Entities" (Section 5).

   MDI instance:  The collection of element values for one entity, as a
      publisher provides it or as a consumer obtains it (Section 5).  A
      consumer's instance takes each value from the discovery response,
      known context, or a binding rule.

   Consumer:  The party that interprets an MDI instance, typically the
      client that performed discovery.

3.  Scope and Non-Goals

   This document defines the abstract content of the MDI common header,
   and proposes how it is derived (Section 6), within the initial
   discovery that the DAWN charter scopes [DAWN-CHARTER].  It does NOT
   define:

   *  a discovery, query, resolution, or transport protocol, and it does
      not bind MDI to DNS or to any other carrier;

   *  a wire format, serialization, or schema; representations are
      illustrative (Appendix A);
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 5]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
*  a new capability card, or a replacement for any existing
      descriptor;

   *  identity verification, authentication, authorization, policy
      evaluation, or attestation procedures;

   *  capability exchange, negotiation, selection, registration, or
      invocation semantics;

   *  open-ended search, semantic matchmaking, ranking, indexing or
      searching across the wider Internet, or trust management and trust
      evaluation beyond the secure exchange of discovery information,
      all of which the charter places out of scope.

4.  Design Principles

   Encoding-neutral model:  Per [RFC3444], one information model can be
      carried by many concrete data models -- a JSON object, a CBOR map,
      a set of DNS records.  Those are carriers; MDI is the shared
      meaning they carry.

   Information, not fields:  Information that initial discovery needs
      can be carried in a discovery response, supplied by known context
      (such as the scope searched or the name the client started from),
      or fixed by a binding.  Necessary information therefore need not
      be repeated in every record.

   Thin core, rich references:  MDI is a header.  Rich or sensitive
      structures -- capability cards, proofs, certificates, policy
      documents -- are referenced, not embedded, and fetched only when
      needed.  A binding can carry small items beyond the minimum, such
      as coarse capability labels, directly as an Extension
      (Section 8.3).

   Discovery-time only:  MDI carries what lets a discovering party
      identify an entity, decide whether follow-up is worthwhile, and
      dereference the next object safely.

   Freshness and rate of change matter -- elements are marked static,
   mainly-static, or dynamic, and validity information is first-class --
   but detailed cache behavior belongs to bindings (Section 9).

5.  Information in the Discovery Flow

   Discovery moves information through four stages, and each stage holds
   different information:

   Publication:  A publisher makes information about an entity available
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 6]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
to a discovery system.  The publisher can be the entity or a
      system that maintains information on its behalf, such as an
      orchestration system [I-D.dunbar-dawn-infra-assisted-discovery].
      Published information contains the published MDI instance and can
      include more, such as administrative or policy data that is never
      returned to clients.

   Serving:  A system that answers lookups, such as an authoritative DNS
      server, a registry, a catalog, or a responder on the local link,
      holds the published information.  It can generate or transform
      parts of the output, for example when an mDNS responder renames an
      instance after a name conflict or a Discovery Proxy answers
      unicast queries with the results of mDNS queries
      [I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns], and it can add information
      of its own or hold different views for different audiences.  A
      serving system outside the publisher's administrative domain, such
      as a gateway or a third-party catalog, is an intermediary.

   Lookup input:  The MVD: the data the discovery mechanism needs to
      return results.  The client supplies some of it in its query, such
      as a name, a protocol, or a scope; other mechanisms can supply the
      rest, such as context derived from the client's network
      attachment.  Depending on the mechanism, a lookup can enumerate a
      scope or target a narrower set.

   Response:  The MDI instances returned for the entities found,
      interpreted together with known context and binding rules.

   +-----------+  published MDI and more   +-----------------+
   | publisher |-------------------------->|                 |
   +-----------+                           |  serving system |
                                           |                 |
   +-----------+   lookup input (MVD)      |  can generate,  |
   |  client   |-------------------------->|  transform, or  |
   |           |<--------------------------|  add            |
   +-----------+   response (MDI)          |  information    |
         |                                 +-----------------+
         |  follow-up at an Endpoint: description retrieval,
         v  interaction, or further discovery

   This document proposes that MDI be considered at publication as well
   as in the response.  The individual terminology draft
   [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology] defines MDI by what an entity needs to
   provide to become discoverable and describes a lookup that returns
   it, and the individual requirements draft
   [I-D.king-dawn-requirements] asks each mechanism to define the MDI
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 7]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
"that every discoverable entity provides"; the charter defines
   discovery by what a client obtains.  The model of Section 8 describes
   MDI instances at both points: published MDI instances, which
   publishers provide, and the instances consumers obtain.  The
   correspondence need not be one to one.  One published MDI instance
   can yield several obtained instances, as when a Discovery Proxy
   relays an advertisement (Section 7), and an entity can have several
   published MDI instances, per protocol or per publisher; a binding
   states how they correspond.  A published and an obtained instance
   have the same elements and can differ in which values they carry and
   in the values themselves: a serving system can generate or transform
   values, as in the examples above; a binding can fix a value, such as
   an Endpoint purpose; and known context can supply one, such as a
   protocol named in the query.  Every other value comes from a
   publisher.

   Considered at publication, the analysis of Section 6 also indicates
   what a publisher has to provide, which each binding states
   (Section 9).  Origin and attribution are assessed per value.  A value
   can be verified to the name that a validated key is authorized for,
   the zone for DNSSEC or the origin for TLS, whoever operates the
   server.  Protection applied at publication, such as a signature by a
   key that the binding ties to the publisher's name through an
   authenticated property of the carrier, covers only values that
   serving systems pass on unchanged.  A value that a serving system
   generates or transforms, as a catalog or a Discovery Proxy does, can
   be verified at most to that serving system, and only where that
   system is authenticated; Section 7 applies this to the deployment
   contexts.  A consumer generally obtains only its own instance, so a
   binding states which values a serving system can generate or
   transform, and those values are attributable to the serving system
   unless protection applied at publication covers them.

   How published information reaches a serving system, for example by
   zone provisioning, DNS Update, SRP registration, or a registry
   interface, is a registration mechanism.
   [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology] lists registration as a function
   separate from the discovery lookup and places it out of scope for
   DAWN, while noting that "the information that can be discovered and
   the trust with which that information is treated are key to any
   complete system".  This document defines no registration mechanism
   (Section 3).

   The MVD and MDI can share attributes, such as a protocol that a
   client asks for and a response reports, and each has items the other
   lacks, such as a search scope in the input and endpoints in MDI.  The
   sources of the input, how it is conveyed, and how it corresponds to
   published information are stated for each deployment context and
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 8]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
binding.  A catalog reached through an Endpoint whose purpose is
   continuing discovery can return MDI instances for the resources it
   lists; how it selects them is outside this document (Section 3).  The
   individual use-cases draft [I-D.kay-dawn-use-cases] leaves to the
   DAWN architecture which query properties belong in the minimum
   (Section 13).

6.  Determining What Is Minimal

   The charter gives examples of the minimum public discovery
   information (type, reachability information, communication protocol,
   and services) but no list [DAWN-CHARTER].  This document uses one
   question to decide what belongs: what does a client need to know to
   use a discovery result?  Using a result means the subsequent
   interactions that the charter names, "such as selection, invocation,
   or communication": choosing one result, then fetching its
   description, talking to it, or asking a catalog for more results.

   An example shows how the question works.  A client on a shop's
   network looks for agents and receives three results.  To choose one
   and talk to it, the client has to tell the three apart, so each
   result needs a name.  It has to know where to connect, such as a host
   and port or a URL, and which agent protocol to speak there, such as
   A2A or MCP.  It has to know what it will find there: the agent
   itself, or a description of the agent, such as an agent card.  And it
   has to know whether the result is still current.  Other information
   helps only in some situations: a short description when a person, not
   software, makes the choice; an authentication scheme when the client
   talks to the agent directly without reading a description first; and
   a stable identifier when the client wants to recognize the same agent
   later or in another result.  The rest, such as the full list of what
   the agent can do, can be read from its description after the choice
   is made, and is not part of the minimum.

   Not everything a client needs has to be in every result.  A client
   that asked for A2A agents already knows the protocol, and a discovery
   mechanism can fix defaults, such as where an agent card is found.
   For each piece of information, this document therefore records two
   things: whether a client needs it, and where the client gets it,
   which is from the result, from its own query, from the rules of the
   discovery mechanism, or later from a description.

   The analysis has four steps:

   1.  List the candidates: the charter's examples and the information
       that existing discovery mechanisms carry (Appendix B).
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                   [Page 9]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
2.  For each candidate and each deployment context of the charter,
       ask: if this were missing, could the client still use the result?
       If not, the client needs it in that context.

   3.  For each candidate the client needs, ask whether the client can
       know it without the result telling it.  If not, results have to
       carry it, and the publisher provides it unless a serving system
       generates it (Section 5).

   4.  Where a candidate is needed only in some situations, state the
       situation.

   What existing mechanisms carry is evidence for step 1: information
   that all of them carry can be required at little cost, and
   information that only some of them carry still has to pass step 2.
   The charter also requires integrity of all discovery information
   exchanged; protecting it, and the origin of the information where
   Table 1 marks this, is an obligation on the binding in every context
   (Section 9).

7.  Applying the Procedure

   This section applies Section 6 to the three deployment contexts of
   the charter.  The charter's examples enumerate all agents in a scope;
   a lookup can also return a narrower set, such as the agents that
   speak one protocol, and the analysis applies to each result in the
   same way.  The DNS-based mechanisms below are representative; the
   same model applies to registry and catalog interfaces.

   Local network:  The charter's example is "discover all agents in this
      shop".  The client knows only its network attachment and possibly
      the agent protocol it wants; it has no name, registry, or prior
      relationship.  DNS-SD over multicast DNS, as the individual draft
      [I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns] profiles it for agents, is a
      representative mechanism.  The next step is to tell the candidates
      apart, choose one, and retrieve its description or interact with
      it.

   Within an organization:  The charter's example is "discover all
      agents available to employees within their employer's
      infrastructure".  In the case tested here, the client is attached
      to the organization's network, possibly through a VPN, and can
      learn from that attachment where to look: DNS-SD domain
      enumeration queries names derived from a domain name received
      through DHCP and from the client's address prefixes ([RFC8766],
      Section 5.2).  Representative mechanisms extend those of the other
      two contexts: DNS-SD beyond the link, through Discovery Proxies
      [RFC8766] or registration with the Service Registration Protocol
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 10]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
(SRP) [RFC9665], as [I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns]
      describes for enterprise campus and branch networks, and DNS
      records in internal zones, which the individual draft
      [I-D.mozleywilliams-dnsop-dnsaid] provides for.  The next step is
      to choose an agent and retrieve its description or interact with
      it, or to continue discovery at a catalog.

   Known domain of a cooperating party:  The charter's example is
      "discover all agents of example.org, for all its customers/
      members", between cooperating parties with trust relationships
      over the public Internet.  The client knows the party's domain
      name and possibly the protocol, and has an organizational trust
      relationship; knowing the name does not show that information came
      from that party, and "for all its customers/members" names an
      audience for the results.  DNS records under the party's domain,
      as proposed in the individual drafts
      [I-D.mozleywilliams-dnsop-dnsaid] and
      [I-D.blwm-dawn-common-discovery-parameters], are representative.
      To find all of a domain's agents, these drafts locate an
      organizational index at a well-known name such as
      _index._agents.<domain>, so the next step is further discovery at
      that index; a client that already knows an agent's name queries

8.  MDI Information Model

   An MDI instance is the common header published or obtained for one
   entity.  Its elements follow the results of Section 7 (Table 1):

   *  Information needed in every context becomes the mandatory Name and
      Endpoint (location, agent protocol, and purpose) and the Freshness
      element.  Freshness is SHOULD because its meaning is obtainable
      from the carrier lifetime; a binding whose carrier provides no
      lifetime MUST carry it.

   *  Information needed only under a condition becomes an element with
      Status MAY: Entity Type, Display Name, Authentication Hint, and
      Stable Identifier.  A publisher cannot always know whether the
      condition holds for a given consumer; a binding or profile for
      deployments where it holds can make the element MUST; for example,
      a binding for exchange between gateways or catalogs can make the
      Stable Identifier MUST.

   *  Services, left to the working group, are covered by Endpoint
      protocols and Entity Type (Section 8.4).

   *  Origin is a protection goal, met by the binding (Section 9), and
      is not an element.

   *  Information beyond the minimum is not an element of MDI.  A
      binding can still carry it as an Extension, and Section 8.3 names
      the kinds that existing formats and individual drafts carry, so
      that bindings agree on their meaning.

   Mandatory elements keep the record very small; in constrained
   carriers, a binding can derive their values from the carrier or from
   known context (Section 9).  All Status values are provisional.
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 14]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
Status key words in this document place obligations on the publishers
   and bindings that claim to carry MDI, and key words addressed to
   consumers apply to implementations of a binding.  Status says how
   strongly the meaning of an element has to be obtainable by the
   consumer, from the response, from known context, or from binding
   rules; whether each response carries a value explicitly is a binding
   decision.  Status applies to the instance a consumer obtains; a
   publisher provides every value that the binding neither derives nor
   fixes and that no serving system generates (Section 5).  Cardinality
   governs how many values of an element may be carried.  Obligations
   specific to binding documents are collected in Section 9.

   The following elements are needed in every context.

   Name:  The name under which a result is found, such as a DNS-SD
      service instance name or a DNS owner name, which lets a client
      tell results apart and refer back to them.  It is often implicit
      in the carrier and is interpreted within its naming scope
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 16]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
(Section 8.5).  One resource can be found under several Names, for
      example one per protocol, and a Name can change, as when multicast
      DNS renames an instance after a conflict.  A Name that the binding
      states to be stable also serves as the Stable Identifier; such a
      name is the stable name by which [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology]
      identifies a Named Entity.

   Endpoint:  Where follow-up can proceed, for what purpose, and in
      which agent protocol; the locator is the charter's "reachability
      information", and the protocols member its "communication
      protocol".  In this document, "agent protocol" means the
      application protocol that an AI resource speaks at the endpoint,
      such as A2A or MCP, whether or not the resource is an agent.  The
      locator-type and locator members say where to go; a binding can
      carry the locator as one value, such as a URI, or assemble it from
      several fields, such as a target host and port plus a path.  The
      protocols member names the agent protocols reached there, such as
      A2A or MCP; the protocol negotiated on the underlying TLS or QUIC
      connection, such as an HTTP version identified by ALPN [RFC7301],
      is different information unless the agent protocol runs directly
      over TLS or QUIC.  A protocols value can name a version or
      versions of the agent protocol; where it does not, the binding
      states how a client learns which version to use, for example from
      the description or through the protocol's own negotiation.  The
      purpose member says what the follow-up is: retrieving a
      description of the resource (for example, an agent card),
      interacting with the resource, or continuing discovery at another
      discovery service, such as a catalog.  When the purpose is not
      carried, the binding states how it follows from the protocol.  An
      Endpoint for description retrieval is how MDI points to a fuller
      capability description, such as a Capability Card; its protocols
      member names the agent protocol of the described resource, the
      description is fetched as the locator implies (for an https URI,
      with an HTTP GET), and the Endpoint can carry a digest of the
      description.  An entity can have Endpoints with different
      protocols and purposes.

   Freshness:  Version, validity window, and refresh hint for the
      instance.  A version need not change when the resource's
      description changes; the digest of a description Endpoint changes
      when the description does (Section 8.5).

   The following elements are needed under the conditions that Section 7
   states.

   Entity Type:  The kind of AI resource: for example an individual

named agent or a service such as a catalog that furthers
      discovery, the two kinds the charter names [DAWN-CHARTER], or
      another AI resource such as an AI inference service
      [I-D.farrel-dawn-terminology].  It lets a client triage before
      fetching anything, and is needed when the client has to tell kinds
      of AI resource apart to choose its next step.  It is usually
      implied by the carrier, such as a well-known name, or by the
      Endpoint purpose, and the binding states how.  The value space is
      left to DAWN, bindings, or profiles to define.

   Display Name:  A short human-readable name or description for a
      person choosing among results, with no machine semantics.  In DNS-
      SD the Name often serves; under unicast owner names, a binding
      carries one, as the title parameter of
      [I-D.blwm-dawn-common-discovery-parameters] does, or takes it from
      the description.  Language handling, if any, is a binding matter.

   Authentication Hint:  The authentication scheme(s) a discovering
      party would need for follow-up, optionally with a Reference to
      authentication metadata.  Never credentials, never authorization
      logic.

   Stable Identifier:  A name for the resource itself that stays the
      same across results, protocols, and time within its naming scope,
      so that a client can correlate results and recognize the resource
      later.  Where the naming scope is itself globally unique, such as
      a DNS domain or a URN namespace, the Stable Identifier is globally
      unique.  Like a Name, it is a name, not a verified identity, key,
      or proof.

   Extension:  An extension named by a binding, profile, or future
      registry, carrying type-specific or ecosystem-specific
      information, including the information beyond the minimum of
      Section 8.3.  An Extension does not alter core element semantics
      and does not reintroduce a non-goal of Section 3.

8.3.  Information Beyond the Minimum

   Table 1 finds that a client does not need the following information
   for its next step, so it is not part of MDI; REQ-PROP-1 of the
   individual requirements draft [I-D.king-dawn-requirements] likewise
   states that "Properties beyond the MDI are optional".  Existing
   formats and individual drafts carry it (Appendix B).  A binding that
   carries it does so as an Extension, with the following meanings:

   Capability Summary:  Coarse, machine-readable capability tags for
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 18]

discovery-time filtering and triage; they are not sufficient for
      selection, negotiation, authorization, or invocation.  The value
      space is not yet defined (Section 8.4).

   Trust Reference:  A Reference to a trust indicator, such as a
      signature, certificate, attestation, trust manifest, or key set.

   Provenance:  Who published this discovery information and,
      optionally, the party responsible for the entity -- information-
      layer provenance, not verified identity.

   Scope Hint:  An opaque hint about discovery scope, audience,
      jurisdiction, or visibility.  It is not access control
      (Section 11).

   Operational Hint:  A coarse availability or health class, or a
      Reference to richer operational state, or both.

8.4.  Services

   The charter lists "services" among its examples [DAWN-CHARTER], and
   REQ-PROP-1 of [I-D.king-dawn-requirements] requires the MDI to
   include "the services it offers"; neither says what a service is.
   This document covers services with existing elements, so there is no
   separate Services element:

   *  The agent protocols a resource offers, such as A2A or MCP, are the
      protocols of its Endpoints.  The per-protocol DNS-SD subtypes
      proposed for agents, such as "_a2a._sub._agents._tcp", name these
      protocols [I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns].

   *  A resource that is itself a service, such as a catalog that
      furthers discovery, as the charter's scope notes, is identified by
      its Entity Type and by an Endpoint whose purpose is continuing
      discovery.

   Coarse labels for what a resource does are the Capability Summary,
   which is beyond the minimum (Section 8.3).  The individual draft
   [I-D.zhang-dawn-bgp-style-federated-discovery] makes such labels
   mandatory, while [I-D.blwm-dawn-common-discovery-parameters] leaves
   capabilities out of DNS records and notes that coarse categories, if
   ever needed, could use DNS-SD subtypes.  A shared vocabulary for the
   labels does not yet exist, and labels used for more than triage would
   be capability information beyond the minimum, which the charter
   places out of scope.  Whether this reading of "services" is right is
   asked in Section 13.

8.5.  Minimal Constraints

   The following rules are part of the model.  A binding MAY tighten
   them but MUST NOT relax them.

   1.  An MDI instance describes exactly one entity.  A discovery
       response may carry information for several entities; aggregation
       is a carrier matter.

   2.  Each Name and Stable Identifier is interpreted within a naming
       scope, which by default is the publisher scope.  A binding states
       the naming scope and stability period of each Name and Stable
       Identifier it carries and how values are compared, with exact
       match as carried as the default.  A Name distinguishes results
       only within its naming scope and stability period, and one
       resource may be found under several Names.  The host name at
       which an endpoint is reached (such as an SVCB TargetName) belongs
       to the Endpoint locator and names a host, even where the same
       string is also a Name.  Correlating results to one resource
       without a Stable Identifier is heuristic.  Within one naming
       scope, equal Stable Identifiers denote the same resource; unequal
       ones do not by themselves denote distinct resources, and across
       naming scopes this model makes no sameness claim.  Where a
       consumer holds several instances with the same Stable Identifier,
       it reconciles them by Freshness only among instances whose
       integrity and origin in that naming scope have been verified as
       the binding defines, using only Freshness values that the same
       verification covers; an instance that has not passed those checks
       does not displace one that has, and other cases are left to the
       binding.

   3.  Protocols attach to endpoints, not to the instance as a whole.  A
       protocols value names an agent protocol, not the protocol
       negotiated on the underlying connection, such as an HTTP version,
       except where the two coincide (Section 8.2).  An instance-level
       protocol list, where a binding offers one, is derived information
       (the union over all endpoints) and cannot diverge from them.  The
       model attaches no meaning to the order of Endpoints; preference
       and selection are binding or carrier matters.

   4.  A digest, when present, is a member of the Reference or of the
       description-retrieval Endpoint it protects and covers exactly the
       object located there.  Integrity is not trust: a matching digest
       shows that the bytes are intact, provided the digest itself was
       obtained with integrity, and it does not show that the publisher
       or the entity should be believed.  A consumer MUST NOT use an
       object whose digest does not match.  How a digest value
       identifies its algorithm is defined by the binding.
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 20]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
5.  A consumer MUST ignore unrecognized Extensions; their presence is
       not an error.  As an exception, a binding MAY mark an Extension
       as mandatory, as SVCB does with its "mandatory" key ([RFC9460]);
       unless the binding says otherwise, a consumer that does not
       understand a mandatory Extension does not use the Endpoint or
       result that carries it, as an SVCB client skips a record that
       lists a mandatory key it does not support.

9.  Binding Expectations

   MDI reaches the wire through bindings: documents that map this model
   onto a concrete encoding or discovery mechanism.  Existing discovery
   formats already approximate bindings (Appendix B); normative bindings
   are expected in separate documents.  A binding that carries MDI MUST
   specify:

   Encoding:  How each element is encoded and which value space it uses,
      defined by the binding, a profile, or a future registry.  Which
      values are implicit and how they are derived from the carrier or
      from known context, for example a Name taken from a DNS-SD service
      instance name; an implicit value counts exactly as an explicit
      one.  How unrecognized Extensions are carried and skipped at the
      encoding level, and which Extensions, if any, are mandatory.

   Names and Endpoints:  The naming scope and stability period of each
      Name and Stable Identifier it carries, and whether it carries a
      Stable Identifier at all.  For each Endpoint, how the locator is
      assembled, how the purpose is determined when it is not carried,
      how protocol versions are expressed or learned, and, where
      connection-level protocols are also carried, how they are kept
      apart from the protocols member.  For an Endpoint for description
      retrieval, which object a digest covers.  For Endpoints whose
      purpose is continuing discovery, the maximum referral depth, how
      loops are detected, and the naming scope of the results obtained
      there.

   Integrity and origin:  How the integrity and origin of the MDI it
      conveys are protected, including how carried values are bound to
      the carrier fields and known context they are interpreted with,
      for example that a response answers the name or scope the client
      asked about, and where verification material comes from.  How the
      publisher scope is determined from an authenticated property of
      the carrier, such as a DNSSEC-validated owner name or a TLS-
      authenticated web origin, never from values the instance asserts
      about itself, such as Provenance, and what publisher scope, if
      any, applies where a serving system generates the content from
      unauthenticated sources, as a Discovery Proxy does.  Whether
      information relayed by a catalog or other intermediary is verified
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 21]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
to the original publisher or only to the intermediary.  How a
      consumer treats failed verification, removed elements, and expired
      information, and what successful verification establishes and what
      it does not.

   Sources and lifetimes of values:  Which values the publisher provides
      and which a serving system generates or transforms, how a consumer
      tells them apart, how what an intermediary adds or changes is
      attributed to it, and which publisher an obtained instance derives
      from when several provide information for one entity.  How carrier
      lifetimes, such as a DNS TTL or an HTTP cache lifetime, combine
      with Freshness, including which record lifetimes apply when a
      result is assembled from several records, with the earliest
      applicable expiry prevailing, and how stale or replayed
      information is detected.

   A binding MUST protect the integrity of all MDI it conveys, as the
   charter requires [DAWN-CHARTER]; what meets this requirement where
   the carrier does not authenticate advertisements is asked in
   Section 13.  A binding MAY tighten Status, cardinality, or
   constraints, including per entity type, but MUST NOT relax a MUST
   element or a rule of Section 8.5.

10.  Security Considerations

   MDI is advertised information; a consumer MUST treat it as unverified
   until corroborated by mechanisms outside this document.

   *  Values prove nothing by themselves.  Any value can be forged by
      whoever publishes or serves it: a Name or Stable Identifier is not
      proof of identity, and an Endpoint does not prove that the
      reachable party is the intended entity.  A matching digest shows
      only that a referenced object is the one the digest was computed
      over, and only if the digest was obtained with integrity; it does
      not show that the publisher or the entity can be trusted.  A
      Display Name or other text chosen by the publisher can imitate
      another entity, including through visually confusable characters,
      or carry text aimed at automated consumers; a client presents such
      text as unverified, does not use it to identify or authorize, and
      does not treat it as instructions.  An Authentication Hint, and
      any metadata at its locator, is unverified; it does not lead a
      client to weaken its authentication requirements or to send
      credentials to a party named only by unverified MDI.  A Trust
      Reference locates assurance material but confers none until that
      material is verified by procedures defined elsewhere.
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 22]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
*  Protection comes from the binding and the carrier, value by value.
      Integrity and origin, including protection against the removal of
      optional elements, are properties of the binding and carrier (for
      example DNSSEC, TLS); each binding states how it provides them and
      to whom origin is verified (Section 9).  A serving system can
      generate, transform, withhold, or replay values, and protection
      applied at publication covers only the values it protects.  A
      value derived from the carrier is as trustworthy as that carrier,
      and a value taken from known context is no more trustworthy than
      that context.  A consumer SHOULD NOT draw conclusions from the
      absence of an element.  A referral to another discovery service
      does not extend the referring publisher's naming scope or
      protection to the results obtained there.

   *  Every locator in MDI, whether of an Endpoint, a Reference, or
      authentication metadata, is supplied by an unverified party, and
      following it carries the usual risks of contacting an untrusted
      party.  The usual dereferencing protections apply, such as limits
      on redirects and response sizes.  Which addresses a client
      connects to is a matter of deployment policy, applied to the
      addresses a locator resolves to at connection time, including
      after redirects.  Link-local and internal addresses are expected
      results of local and organizational discovery; unless deployment
      policy permits otherwise, a client does not connect to them when
      they appear in information obtained from outside that context.

11.  Privacy Considerations

   Publishing MDI can reveal entity names, endpoints, organizational
   structure, capability hints, and scope information.  Publishers
   SHOULD minimize public MDI to what discovery requires and SHOULD
   place sensitive information -- detailed capabilities, policy,
   internal topology, operational state -- behind access-controlled
   references.

   A Stable Identifier lets any observer correlate an entity across
   results, protocols, and time.  Where an entity acts for an
   individual, its names, identifiers, and Provenance can be personal
   data; publishers that do not need correlation can omit a Stable
   Identifier.  A Scope Hint is not access control.

   Published information, including data never returned to clients, is
   disclosed to every serving system that holds it, and views for
   different audiences stay separate only as far as that system keeps
   them apart.  A key used to sign published MDI instances can be
   tracked like an identifier ([RFC9665], Section 7): a key unique to an
   entity correlates that entity as a Stable Identifier does, and a key
   shared by a publisher links the entities it publishes.
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 23]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
Results obtained under an audience restriction carry no marker of it;
   a cache, intermediary, or consumer that passes them outside that
   audience discloses internal names, endpoints, and topology.  A
   Discovery Proxy makes advertisements visible to whoever can query it,
   and the domain a result appears under can reveal where a device, and
   a person it acts for, is ([RFC8766], Section 9.2).

12.  IANA Considerations

   Future revisions or binding documents may request registries for
   selected MDI value spaces, such as entity types or protocol
   identifiers; existing registries are to be reused where applicable.

13.  Open Issues

   Input from the working group is sought on the following questions:

   *  Is the procedure of Section 6 an acceptable basis for deciding
      what belongs in the minimum public discovery information?  Are the
      results of Section 7 right, including the conditions under which
      an entity type, a display name, an authentication hint, or a
      Stable Identifier is needed, and is the information that Table 1
      places beyond the minimum correctly left out of MDI?  If so,
      should this document keep naming that information for bindings
      (Section 8.3), or leave it to bindings and profiles?

   *  Should MDI be defined only by what a client obtains, or also at
      publication?  This document proposes both points, with Status
      defined for the instance a consumer obtains and a publisher's
      obligations derived from it per binding (Section 5); an
      alternative is to set Status separately for published MDI
      instances.

   *  Which items of the MVD should DAWN specify for each deployment
      context?  Is requester context, such as the audience that
      "available to employees" or "for all its customers/members"
      implies, part of the MVD, and if so who supplies it, or is it
      access control outside discovery?  Is information restricted to
      such an audience public in the charter's sense?

   *  How should the charter's examples "type" and "services" be read?
      This document makes Entity Type conditional and usually implied by
      the carrier or the Endpoint purpose, covers services by the
      protocols of Endpoints and by Entity Type, and places coarse
      labels (Capability Summary) beyond the minimum (Section 8.4); is
      that right?
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 24]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
*  Where an agent's advertisement or registration is not
      authenticated to its operator, as with multicast DNS, a Discovery
      Proxy relaying it, or SRP, how is the charter's requirement for
      integrity of the information exchanged met: by authenticating the
      agent at the follow-up connection, by that together with
      protection of the hop that delivers the information, such as a
      Discovery Proxy's DNSSEC signature, or only by protecting the
      advertisement to its origin, for example with authenticated DNS
      Update ([RFC8766], Section 9.1), which places a requirement on
      registration (Section 5)?  See Section 7.

   In the DNS and DNS-SD column, proto carries the agent protocol; the
   alpn parameter carries the protocol negotiated on the TLS or QUIC
   connection and corresponds to no MDI element.
   [I-D.mozleywilliams-dnsop-dnsaid] carries the agent protocol in alpn
   or in a separate bap parameter.

   Each record carries one proto value, so a resource that offers
   several protocols appears in several records: in DNS-SD each has its
   own instance name, while unicast SVCB records share one owner name.
   Each record supplies the Endpoint for its protocol, and a desc-ref
Cui & Yao                  Expires 6 May 2027                  [Page 29]
Internet-Draft    Minimum Public Discovery Information     November 2026
supplies an Endpoint for description retrieval, whose description can
   be hosted elsewhere.  [I-D.blwm-dawn-common-discovery-parameters] and
   [I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns] infer from a shared desc-ref
   that records belong to one agent;
   [I-D.blwm-dawn-common-discovery-parameters] states that desc-ref is
   neither a universal identifier nor authenticated.  The desc-ref-
   digest parameter is defined as a freshness signal, not a security
   signal, and maps to Freshness; it is not the digest of the
   description Endpoint (rule 4 of Section 8.5).

   The locator is assembled from the SRV or SVCB target and port plus
   the path or well-known parameter or, absent both, the protocol's
   default location (for A2A, /.well-known/agent-card.json).  Whether
   the advertised location serves description retrieval or interaction
   follows from the protocol: for A2A it serves the agent card, from
   which interaction URLs are taken, while for MCP it is the interaction
   endpoint [I-D.jakab-dawn-agent-discovery-mdns].

   The individual drafts [I-D.zhang-dawn-bgp-style-federated-discovery]
   and [I-D.zhang-dawn-agent-discovery-framework] define a TLV binding
   of MDI elements for exchange between gateways, including an Entity-
   Identifier and a Capability-Summary.  In
   [I-D.zhang-dawn-bgp-style-federated-discovery], the Entity-
   Identifier, which together with the Origin-GW-ID keys a record, can
   serve as the Name and corresponds to the Stable Identifier; the
   Capability-Card-URI is an Endpoint for description retrieval; and the
   Capability-Summary, Trust-Reference, and Provenance TLVs correspond
   to the information of the same names beyond the minimum
   (Section 8.3).  That draft makes the Capability-Summary mandatory,
   while Table 1 places coarse capability labels beyond the minimum.
